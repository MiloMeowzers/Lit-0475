How to use this guide

Every event, claim and quotation in this guide is tied to the play. References take the form I.202: Act I, line 202, where each line of dialogue counts as one line, however long. A stage direction is referenced to the line it follows, for example I.274 s.d.. Lines are counted from the start of each act in the Project Gutenberg text of the play (eBook #844), the three-act version used by most school editions. Act I has 301 lines of dialogue, Act II has 397 and Act III has 181.

Your own edition will have page numbers rather than line numbers, so each reference sits beside a description of the moment. Use the line number to judge position: II.300 is about three-quarters of the way through Act II.

Two ways to read the banks. The event bank, quote bank and theme arcs can be read by act, for revising a single section of the play, or as a whole arc, for essay questions that range across the play. Each theme section ends with a one-line summary of its arc and the numbers of the quotations that track it through Acts I, II and III.

Context

Facts about Wilde and the first production come from published study guides, each one named. Facts about the play come from the play.

The first performance

The cast list printed with the play records the first production at the St James’s Theatre, London, on 14 February 1895, with George Alexander (also the theatre’s manager) as John Worthing.

Source: Play text, front matter (cast list)

Writing and revision

Wilde wrote the play in the summer of 1894 at Worthing, the seaside town that gives Jack his surname. It was first written in four acts; at Alexander’s request it was cut to three by omitting “the Gribsby episode” and merging two acts into one.

Source: LitCharts, Key Facts p. 1; CliffsNotes, “About the play” and Wilde biography

The play inside its own world

Jack himself explains that Worthing is “a place in Sussex . . . a seaside resort” I.205, the scene list sets the action in “TIME: The Present”, and Lady Bracknell measures time by the London season I.216.

Source: Play text (front matter and Act I)

Aestheticism

Wilde was associated with the Aesthetic movement and its idea of “art for art’s sake”, learned partly from Walter Pater at Oxford. The play’s preference for style over sincerity III.19 can be read in this light.

Source: LitCharts, Brief Biography p. 1

Genre: farce, comedy of manners and melodrama

Wilde borrowed from French farce and the “well-made play”, with their mistaken identities, lost children and secrets from the past, and from the Victorian comedy of manners. The guides describe the play as a mixture of satire, comedy of manners and farce.

Source: SparkNotes, Context; CliffsNotes, “About the play”

The dandy

The dandy, a witty, carefully dressed figure who speaks in epigrams and mocks moral cant, is a recurring Wilde type. Algernon is the clearest example in this play.

Source: SparkNotes, Context and Motifs

Scandal and trial

On 28 February 1895, two weeks after the opening, the Marquess of Queensberry left a card at Wilde’s club accusing him of “posing as a sodomite” (misspelled). Wilde sued for libel and lost, was then prosecuted for gross indecency under the Criminal Law Amendment Act of 1885, convicted and sentenced to two years’ hard labour. The production was withdrawn. Sources give different figures for the length of the first run, so the guide does not state one.

Source: CliffsNotes, “Disaster and Ruin”; SparkNotes, Context; LitCharts, Historical Context

Later readings

SparkNotes notes that the play was long seen as purely frivolous, and that later critics have read its double lives and talk of “wickedness” as a coded commentary on secrecy and hypocrisy. This is an interpretation, not a fact about Wilde’s intention, and should be presented as such in an answer.

Source: SparkNotes, Context

The plot in brief

Act I. In his London flat Algernon uses a cigarette case to discover that his friend “Ernest” is called Jack in the country, where he is guardian to Cecily I.71, and reveals his own invented invalid, Bunbury I.88. Jack proposes to Gwendolen, who loves him for his name I.153. Lady Bracknell forbids the match when she learns Jack was found in a hand-bag I.218. Algernon secretly notes Jack’s country address I.274 s.d..

Act II. At Jack’s house in Hertfordshire Algernon arrives as Jack’s wicked brother Ernest II.42 and proposes to Cecily, who has imagined their engagement for months II.210. Jack arrives in mourning for the brother who is in the dining-room II.88 s.d.. Gwendolen arrives; the two women find they are both engaged to “Ernest” II.289, and both deceptions are exposed II.349. Each man has arranged to be christened Ernest II.385.

Act III. The women forgive the men on condition that they are christened III.29. Lady Bracknell accepts Algernon’s engagement to Cecily once she hears of Cecily’s fortune III.77, but Jack will not consent unless he may marry Gwendolen III.105. A chance mention of Miss Prism reveals that Jack is the baby she lost in a hand-bag twenty-eight years before III.126: Lady Bracknell’s nephew, Algernon’s elder brother III.153, christened Ernest John after his father III.170.

The play’s own list of scenes gives the three settings; Act III’s stage direction calls the room a “Morning-room” III, opening s.d.. The action covers two days. Act I ends with Algernon planning to go Bunburying “To-morrow” I.288; in Act II Gwendolen dates Jack’s proposal to “yesterday afternoon” II.291; Act III continues the same afternoon III.91. Act II is set in July II, opening s.d..

Event bank

Thirty-one numbered events in order. Read one act for close revision, or read E1 to E31 straight through as the arc of the whole play.

Act I

E1 Algernon and Lane: piano, champagne and marriage I.1–17

Algernon boasts that he plays with “wonderful expression” rather than accuracy, queries the eight bottles of champagne recorded in Lane’s book, and receives Lane’s deadpan account of his own marriage. Alone, he complains that the “lower orders” fail to set a good example I.17.

Why it matters. The first exchange sets the play’s method: master and servant trade composed inversions, and moral responsibility is assigned to the class least able to enforce it.

E2 “Ernest” arrives in town I.18–52

Lane announces “Mr. Ernest Worthing” I.18. Jack says he has come up to town to propose to Gwendolen I.36. Algernon guards the cucumber sandwiches while eating them himself I.41 and refuses consent until Jack explains “the whole question of Cecily” I.51.

Why it matters. The audience meets Jack under the false name first, so the play opens inside the deception it will later resolve.

E3 The cigarette case I.53–78

The inscription “From little Cecily, with her fondest love to her dear Uncle Jack” exposes Jack’s second name I.68. He first claims Cecily is his aunt in Tunbridge Wells I.63 and then admits he is “Ernest in town and Jack in the country” I.71.

Why it matters. A private object becomes public evidence; the first of several written texts in the play (case, card, diary, Army List) that expose or confirm identity.

E4 Jack’s explanation I.79–83

Jack explains that Thomas Cardew adopted him and made him guardian to Cecily, who lives in the country with Miss Prism I.79. To escape the “high moral tone” required of a guardian, he invented a younger brother, Ernest, who lives in the Albany I.83.

Why it matters. Respectability is presented as the cause of deception rather than its opposite.

E5 Bunbury and Bunburying I.84–102

Algernon reveals his own fiction, “an invaluable permanent invalid called Bunbury”, used to escape to the country and to avoid dining with his aunt I.88. Jack resolves to “kill” his brother if Gwendolen accepts him I.93.

Why it matters. The two men are established as mirror images: one escapes to town, the other to the country.

E6 Lady Bracknell and Gwendolen arrive I.103–132

The plate of cucumber sandwiches is empty, though the audience has watched Algernon eat from it I.41. He picks up the plate “in horror” I.115 and Lane supports him with the claim that there were “no cucumbers in the market” I.116. Algernon cites a telegram about Bunbury’s illness to escape dinner I.127, prompting Lady Bracknell’s view that Bunbury should decide “whether he was going to live or to die” I.130.

Why it matters. Lane’s lie in support of his master quietly shows deception running through both classes.

E7 Jack’s proposal I.134–167

Gwendolen confesses that her ideal “has always been to love some one of the name of Ernest” I.141, dismisses the name Jack I.153, and directs the proposal herself, announcing in advance that she will accept I.159.

Why it matters. Romance is conducted as performance and rehearsal; the woman controls the scene that convention gives to the man.

E8 The interview I.168–218

Lady Bracknell forbids the engagement I.172 and questions Jack from her list of “eligible young men” on smoking, age, income, property, politics and parents I.178. Jack reveals that he was found in a hand-bag in the cloak-room at Victoria Station I.211. She advises him to “acquire some relations” I.216 and refuses to let her daughter “marry into a cloak-room, and form an alliance with a parcel” I.218.

Why it matters. The central obstacle of the plot is set: Jack has no family, and therefore no place in Society.

E9 After the interview I.219–264

Algernon plays the Wedding March I.219. Jack calls Lady Bracknell a Gorgon I.221 and plans to kill Ernest off with “a severe chill” in Paris I.245. He lets slip that Cecily is “excessively pretty” and “only just eighteen” I.249.

Why it matters. Jack supplies the very information that sends Algernon to the country.

E10 Gwendolen returns I.266–284

Gwendolen declares her “eternal devotion” whatever her mother does I.271 and asks for Jack’s country address. Algernon writes it on his shirt-cuff I.274 s.d..

Why it matters. A silent piece of stage business plants the next act.

E11 Algernon plans to go Bunburying I.286–301

Algernon tears up letters that appear to be bills I.285 s.d. and announces that he is going Bunburying the next day I.288. The act ends with him reading his shirt-cuff and smiling I.301 s.d..

Why it matters. The act closes on a visual cue that the audience understands and Jack does not: dramatic irony carries the plot into Act II.

Act II

E12 The garden: Cecily, Miss Prism and Dr Chasuble II.1–34

Miss Prism tries to teach Cecily German II.1. Cecily defends her diary II.10, and Miss Prism mentions the three-volume novel she once wrote, whose manuscript “was abandoned” II.17. Dr Chasuble calls and the two go for a walk.

Why it matters. The lost manuscript is a plant for Act III, delivered as a passing joke.

E13 Algernon arrives as “Ernest” II.35–80

Algernon presents Jack’s card II.36 and meets Cecily, who expects her “wicked cousin Ernest” II.42. She tells him Jack plans to send him to Australia II.58 and pins a pink rose in his buttonhole II.77.

Why it matters. Algernon steps into the fiction Jack created; the card that proved Jack was “Ernest” in Act I now lets someone else become him.

E14 Miss Prism and Chasuble on marriage II.81–87

Miss Prism urges Chasuble to marry II.81, and both cover their double meanings by explaining their metaphors II.87.

Why it matters. A comic subplot of desire hidden under propriety, resolved in the final embrace.

E15 Jack in mourning II.88–128

Jack enters in full mourning II.88 s.d. and announces that Ernest has died in Paris of a severe chill II.106. He arranges with Chasuble to be christened at half-past five II.126.

Why it matters. Jack’s fiction and Algernon’s are about to collide; the audience knows “Ernest” is in the dining-room.

E16 The brothers “reconciled” II.129–158

Cecily produces “your brother Ernest” II.134. Jack refuses Algernon’s hand until Cecily threatens never to forgive him II.149.

Why it matters. A visual climax of dramatic irony: two men who know the truth act out a reconciliation for an audience on stage who do not.

E17 Jack orders Algernon away II.159–184

Merriman reports that “Mr. Ernest’s” luggage has been unpacked II.164. Jack orders the dog-cart; Algernon mocks the mourning clothes II.176.

Why it matters. The quarrel is fought through costume and luggage, the props of farce.

E18 Algernon’s proposal and Cecily’s diary II.185–248

Cecily takes down Algernon’s declaration as dictation II.199. She reveals they have been engaged for three months II.210, that she wrote his letters herself II.220, and that she broke off and renewed the engagement in her diary II.224. She too must marry an Ernest II.234. Algernon leaves to arrange a christening II.243.

Why it matters. Cecily is the author of her own romance; the man she accepts has been written in advance.

E19 Gwendolen meets Cecily II.249–297

The two women declare instant friendship II.257, but Gwendolen grows wary on learning Cecily is Mr Worthing’s ward II.279. Each then claims to be engaged to Ernest Worthing, with diaries as evidence II.291.

Why it matters. The parallel proposals of Act I and Act II now meet in a single misunderstanding.

E20 The tea-table quarrel II.298–319

With servants present, the rivals fight through politeness II.297 s.d.. Gwendolen refuses sugar and cake as unfashionable II.312; Cecily gives her both II.312.

Why it matters. Manners become weapons: the quarrel is conducted entirely through the rituals of afternoon tea.

E21 The double exposure II.320–346

Each woman identifies the other’s fiancé by his real name II.325 II.336. The women embrace as allies II.337 and call each other sister II.346.

Why it matters. The scene is built on exact repetition, each line answered by its mirror image. Jack’s Act I prediction that the women would call each other sister is fulfilled I.251.

E22 Jack admits there is no Ernest II.347–357

Asked where his brother Ernest is, Jack admits he has none II.349, and the women withdraw into the house II.357 s.d..

Why it matters. The first time Jack is “forced to speak the truth”, and it ruins both engagements.

E23 The muffin quarrel II.358–397

Algernon eats muffins calmly “because I am unhappy” II.377. Both men reveal they have arranged to be christened Ernest II.385. The act ends with Algernon still eating II.397.

Why it matters. The act ends on appetite rather than resolution, mirroring the cucumber sandwiches of Act I.

Act III

E24 Explanations accepted III.1–39

The women resolve on “a dignified silence” III.7 and break it at once. They accept the men’s explanations for their “beauty” rather than their truth III.18, but the names remain “an insuperable barrier” until the men promise to be christened III.29.

Why it matters. Gwendolen states the play’s governing principle: “style, not sincerity” III.19.

E25 Lady Bracknell arrives; Bunbury dies III.40–56

Lady Bracknell has followed Gwendolen “by a luggage train” III.44 and forbids all communication with Jack III.44. Algernon announces that Bunbury is dead, “quite exploded” III.53.

Why it matters. Algernon’s fiction is killed off as Jack’s was, completing the parallel.

E26 Lady Bracknell examines Cecily III.57–86

Lady Bracknell questions Cecily’s origins III.61. Jack lists addresses, solicitors and certificates; only the news of “a hundred and thirty thousand pounds in the Funds” changes her mind III.70. She approves the match III.77.

Why it matters. The interview of Act I is replayed with the roles reversed: this time Jack holds the information.

E27 Deadlock III.87–108

Jack refuses consent as Cecily’s guardian, citing Algernon’s untruthfulness III.89, and reveals that under her grandfather’s will Cecily does not come of age until thirty-five III.97. He offers a bargain: his consent for Lady Bracknell’s III.105. She refuses III.106.

Why it matters. The comic plot reaches complete stalemate; only an external revelation can free it.

E28 The christenings cancelled; Miss Prism named III.109–124

Lady Bracknell forbids Algernon’s baptism III.112 and Jack withdraws III.114. Chasuble mentions that Miss Prism is waiting in the vestry, and Lady Bracknell reacts at once III.116.

Why it matters. The chance mention of a name triggers the recognition plot.

E29 “Prism! Where is that baby?” III.125–133

Lady Bracknell recalls that twenty-eight years earlier Prism left Lord Bracknell’s house with a baby boy and never returned; the perambulator held only a three-volume novel III.126. Miss Prism explains that she put the manuscript in the basinette and the baby in the hand-bag III.127, which she left at Victoria, the Brighton line III.133.

Why it matters. Act I’s comic interview details (hand-bag, cloak-room, Brighton line) turn out to be the exact evidence of identity.

E30 The hand-bag and the mistaken mother III.134–155

After noise offstage III.137 s.d., Jack produces the hand-bag, which Miss Prism identifies by its marks III.145. Jack embraces her as his mother III.148; she redirects him to Lady Bracknell, who reveals he is the son of her sister, Mrs Moncrieff, and Algernon’s elder brother III.153.

Why it matters. The melodramatic recognition is parodied through a false start before the true one.

E31 The name in the Army Lists III.156–181

Gwendolen will not change her decision on names III.158. Lady Bracknell confirms Jack was christened after his father III.163; the Army Lists give the General’s Christian names as “Ernest John” III.170. Three couples embrace III.176, and Jack delivers the title line III.181.

Why it matters. Jack’s invented name proves to be his real one: the lie turns into the truth without anyone having to become sincere.

Characters

Jack Worthing (John; “Ernest” in town)

Guardian of Cecily, listed in the cast as “John Worthing, J.P.” (Justice of the Peace), owner of the Manor House at Woolton; the foundling whose origin drives the plot.

  • Leads a double life, justified by the “high moral tone” a guardian must adopt I.83.
  • Sincere in love but evasive about truth, which he thinks unsuitable for “a nice, sweet, refined girl” I.237.
  • Often irritable and earnest in manner: he loathes listening, talking and looking I.257 I.259, and stages his brother’s death in full mourning II.88 s.d..
  • Gains power in Act III as guardian, withholding consent III.87, then loses all composure in his search for his name III.170.

Arc. From inventor of a fiction (Act I) to its victim (Act II) to its unwitting fulfilment (Act III).

Algernon Moncrieff

Lady Bracknell’s nephew, a London bachelor and dandy; inventor of Bunbury.

  • Speaks almost entirely in epigrams and inversions I.84 I.228.
  • Defined by appetite: cucumber sandwiches in Act I I.41 and muffins in Act II II.377.
  • Financially insecure, tearing up bills I.285 s.d.; Lady Bracknell admits he “has nothing to depend upon” but debts III.77.
  • Falls in love with Cecily at once II.184 and, unlike Jack, is frank about putting pleasure before duty II.172.

Arc. From cynic about marriage (Act I) to eager fiancé (Act II) to the younger brother of the man he mocked (Act III).

Lady Bracknell (Aunt Augusta)

Gwendolen’s mother and Algernon’s aunt; the play’s gatekeeper of Society and its main obstacle.

  • Speaks in confident, balanced epigrams that make prejudice sound like reason I.202.
  • Treats marriage as a transaction recorded in a notebook I.178 and changes her view of Cecily as soon as money is mentioned III.71.
  • Fears disorder from below: education, revolution and “social legislation” I.184 III.54.
  • Herself married up without fortune III.77, and keeps her husband deceived as a matter of principle III.44.
  • Holds the knowledge that resolves the plot III.126.

Arc. Obstacle in Act I, intruder in Act III, and finally the agent of recognition.

Gwendolen Fairfax

Lady Bracknell’s daughter, engaged to Jack.

  • Determined and self-assured: she accepts Jack before he proposes I.159 and claims never to be wrong I.137.
  • Loves the name Ernest more than the man I.153.
  • Shares her mother’s snobbery and use of the lorgnette at the tea table II.269 II.297.
  • Values performance in life: style over sincerity III.19, and suspense for its own sake III.143.

Arc. Moves from rebelling against her mother’s control (Act I) to rivalry and alliance with Cecily (Act II) to marriage on her own terms (Act III). A useful exam question asks how far she resembles her mother (see the past-paper bank).

Cecily Cardew

Jack’s ward, eighteen, an heiress living in the country under Miss Prism’s care.

  • Dislikes her lessons and prefers her diary II.34 II.10.
  • Fascinated by wickedness II.40.
  • Invents her own romance, including the engagement, the ring and the letters II.216.
  • As sharp as Gwendolen in combat II.312, and impatient of waiting III.101.

Arc. The innocent country girl Jack describes in Act I I.247 proves the most inventive character in the play.

Miss Prism

Cecily’s governess; author of the lost three-volume novel.

  • Stern moraliser II.9 with a hidden romantic interest in Chasuble II.81.
  • Her past is planted in Act II II.17 and revealed in Act III III.127.
  • Mistaken by Jack for his unmarried mother III.148.

Arc. From comic moralist to the source of the plot’s resolution.

Canon Chasuble, D.D.

The local rector.

  • Hides feeling behind learned phrasing II.26.
  • Satirised through his all-purpose sermon II.110 and his unpublished sermons against the Anabaptists III.115.
  • Accepts the christenings without question and is united with Miss Prism at the end III.176.

Arc. A minor comic figure whose casual mention of Miss Prism triggers the recognition III.115.

Lane and Merriman

Algernon’s manservant (town) and Jack’s butler (country).

  • Lane is impassive and witty, and covers for his master’s greed I.116.
  • Merriman’s announcements structure entrances and exits II.35 III.40.
  • The presence of servants restrains the quarrelling women II.297 s.d..

Arc. Silent witnesses to upper-class behaviour, used for timing and irony.

Themes

Each theme is traced through the three acts. Quotation numbers refer to the quote bank in Section 9.

Identity, names and the double life

Every main character in the play is defined by a name, and most of them are wrong about it. Jack is Ernest in town and Jack in the country I.71; Algernon invents Bunbury I.88; both women love a name rather than a man I.141 II.234. The comic question of who someone is turns out to be a question about family and status, and it is answered by documents rather than by character.

Act IAct I sets up the double life. The cigarette case exposes Jack’s second name I.68, Algernon names the practice “Bunburying” I.86, and Lady Bracknell reveals the cost of having no origin: Jack, found in a hand-bag, has no place in Society I.218.
Quotations: Q4, Q8, Q9, Q10, Q12, Q18, Q19, Q20, Q28, Q29, Q30
Act IIIn Act II the fictions multiply and collide. Algernon becomes “Ernest” using Jack’s own card II.36; Jack arrives in mourning for a brother who is in the dining-room II.88 s.d.; and the double exposure strips both men of the name the women love II.338 II.341.
Quotations: Q45, Q54, Q60
Act IIIAct III resolves identity through records. The hand-bag, the Army Lists and Lady Bracknell’s memory establish that Jack is Ernest John Moncrieff, Algernon’s elder brother III.153 III.170. Jack learns that the name he invented was true all along III.173.
Quotations: Q77, Q82, Q94, Q96, Q101, Q104, Q107

The whole arc. Across the play, identity moves from invention (Act I) to confusion (Act II) to documentary proof (Act III). The lie becomes the truth without anyone choosing to be honest.

Marriage, courtship and romance

Marriage drives the plot and is discussed in almost every scene. It is treated as a business arrangement by Lady Bracknell, as a romantic fiction by the young women, and as something to escape by Algernon in Act I.

Act IAct I opens with Lane’s account of his “misunderstanding” I.12 and Algernon’s cynicism I.41. Gwendolen stage-manages Jack’s proposal I.159, and Lady Bracknell turns it into an interview from her list of eligible men I.178.
Quotations: Q2, Q5, Q6, Q13, Q15, Q18, Q19, Q21, Q22, Q23, Q24, Q32
Act IIAct II gives a second proposal that parodies the first: Cecily has already conducted the whole engagement in her diary II.210. Miss Prism courts Chasuble through talk of matrimony II.81, and the two engagements collide at the tea table II.289.
Quotations: Q47, Q48, Q49, Q56, Q57, Q58, Q59, Q60
Act IIIIn Act III marriage becomes a negotiation over money and consent. Cecily’s fortune wins Lady Bracknell’s approval III.71, Jack trades consent for consent III.105, and three couples embrace once the obstacles are removed III.179.
Quotations: Q80, Q84, Q88, Q89, Q93, Q103, Q105

The whole arc. The play moves from talk about marriage to the mechanics of arranging it. Romance is real but always expressed through conventions: proposals rehearsed, diaries kept, consents bargained.

Class, money and Society

The characters are almost all wealthy, and Wilde satirises the values that keep them so: birth, property, fashion and the exclusion of outsiders. Servants are present throughout and often more composed than their employers.

Wilde’s methods

AO3 asks how the writer creates meanings and effects through language, structure and form. In a play, form includes what an audience sees and hears.

Epigram and paradox

Short, balanced, quotable sentences that state a surprising general truth, often by reversing a familiar idea I.84 III.140. Comment on the balance (antithesis), the turn on the final word, and what the line reveals about the speaker.

Inversion

Expected values are turned upside down: the servants should set a moral example I.17, being found is worse than losing parents, truth is a thing to apologise for III.173.

The pun on Ernest / earnest

First voiced by Algernon I.70 and completed in the last line III.181. Discuss both meanings: the name and the moral quality.

Parallel and mirrored scenes

Structure is built on repetition: two invented people (Ernest and Bunbury), two proposals (Act I and Act II), two interviews by Lady Bracknell (Jack in Act I, Cecily in Act III), two christenings booked, two women who say the same lines II.324 II.335. Mirroring makes the action feel mechanical, which is one source of farce.

Stichomythia and rapid exchange

Short alternating lines build pace and comic rhythm, as in the tea-table duel II.311–313 and the women’s rehearsed silence III.7–11.

Stage directions and physical comedy

Wilde’s directions carry jokes that dialogue does not: Algernon writing on his cuff I.274 s.d., Jack’s mourning dress II.88 s.d., Cecily’s four lumps of sugar, trunks being thrown about offstage III.137 s.d.. In a drama answer, always comment on what an audience would see.

Dramatic irony

The audience knows more than the characters, especially in Act II: Cecily warns against a double life to a man living one II.44, and Jack mourns a brother who is in the dining-room II.132.

Melodrama conventions parodied

Lost babies, a mysterious hand-bag, the governess with a past, a mistaken mother and a revelation from a book are stock devices of Victorian melodrama. Wilde uses them straight-faced and lets characters remark on them III.137.

Structure and time

Three acts, three settings (the flat in Half-Moon Street; the garden and then the drawing-room at Woolton) I, opening s.d.. The action is compressed into two days: Jack proposes “yesterday afternoon at 5.30”, according to Gwendolen on the second day II.291, and Act III follows the same afternoon III.91.

Asides and choral speech

Gwendolen’s aside lets the audience hear what politeness hides II.310; the women and men speak in unison III.29 III.30, turning dialogue into a duet.

Motifs and symbols

Food

Cucumber sandwiches and bread and butter (Act I) I.41, sugar, cake and tea (Act II, first half) II.314, muffins and tea-cake (Act II, close) II.393. Food stands in for desire, rivalry and selfishness, and lets characters fight without saying what they mean.

The hand-bag and the railway

Mentioned as a comic absurdity I.207, mocked as an origin III.61, then produced as proof III.143. Victoria Station and the Brighton line are repeated word for word in both acts I.213 III.133.

Diaries, cards and documents

The card proves Jack is “Ernest” in Act I I.70 and introduces Algernon as Ernest in Act II; the diaries supply rival claims II.291; the Army Lists settle the name III.170.

Mourning and christening

Clothes and ceremonies mark changes of identity that never quite happen: Jack’s mourning for an invented death, two christenings arranged and cancelled III.113.

The garden and the town

Jack’s town/country division I.24 is mapped onto the settings. The country garden “full of roses” II, opening s.d. is not innocent: it is where the most elaborate fictions are played out.
